There is a moment, usually about six months after handover, when a client tells
us which part of the house they like most. It is almost never the part that
photographed best.

## Value that reveals itself slowly

Expensive-looking and expensive are not the same instinct. The first is a
performance staged for a visitor who will be in the room for twenty minutes. The
second is a set of decisions that only make sense to someone who lives there.

Gloss, gold and scale all read as value instantly and then stop. A honed stone
surface does the opposite. On the first day it is quiet. By the second year, the
places you touch most have taken on a slight sheen, and the material has begun
to record how the house is used.

> The materials worth paying for are the ones that improve under attention
> rather than collapse under it.

## Restraint is a discipline, not an absence

A narrow palette is harder than a wide one. Three materials repeated at
different scales force every proportion to be correct, because there is nothing
else for the eye to land on.

The moment a fourth material enters, it has to justify itself against the three
already there. Most of the time it cannot, and the room becomes a collection
rather than a composition.

## Detailing survives close inspection

The test we use is simple. Stand at arm's length from any junction in the room.
If the meeting of two materials looks like a decision, it was designed. If it
looks like a tolerance, it was resolved on site.

A four-millimetre shadow gap where oak meets steel costs almost nothing in
material. It costs a great deal in coordination, because it means the joinery
and the metalwork were drawn together and set in the right order. That is the
difference a client feels without being able to name it.

## What this means for a brief

If you are planning a significant interior, the most useful thing you can do
early is decide what the space should feel like on an ordinary Tuesday evening,
not what it should look like when it is full.

Everything else follows from that. The palette narrows on its own. The detailing
becomes obvious. And the result is a room that holds its value long after the
trend that produced it has passed.
