Ask a family what went wrong with their last interior and the answer is almost
never about materials. It is that there was nowhere sensible to put things.

## Clutter is a design failure, not a discipline failure

Every household generates the same objects. Keys, post, bags, chargers, shoes,
medicine, documents, the equipment of whatever the family actually does. These
arrive daily whether or not the plan accounted for them.

If the design gave them a home within a few steps of where they are used, they
go there. If it did not, they accumulate on the nearest horizontal surface,
which is usually the one the design worked hardest on.

## Storage belongs in the plan, not the furniture schedule

The distinction matters more than it sounds. Storage designed at plan stage can
be given depth, height and position. It can take a wall that was going to be
blank anyway. It can be lit, ventilated and reached without moving anything
else.

Storage bought later can only occupy space the plan did not want.

> The wardrobes a family remembers are the ones they never think about.

## Three rules we work to

- **Store where it is used.** A cupboard in the right place at half the size
  beats a generous one in the wrong place.
- **Depth follows contents.** Six hundred millimetres for hanging, three fifty
  for folded, two twenty for most of what actually accumulates.
- **Concealment must be effortless.** A push-latch that needs a second attempt
  will be left open within a month.

## The compounding effect

None of this is visible in a photograph, which is why it is under-designed so
often. But it is the reason one interior still looks composed after five years
and another needs re-styling before the first anniversary.

The materials get the attention. The storage decides whether you ever see them.
